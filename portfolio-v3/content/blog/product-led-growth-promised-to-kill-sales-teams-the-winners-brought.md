*Why most PLG companies see no advantage, and what the winners actually do.*

![Abstract dark glass curtain-wall facade with geometric grid panels and a red accent, representing structured growth systems](/blog/product-led-growth-promised-to-kill-sales-teams-the-winners-brought/hero.jpg)

The Gartner survey landed in March 2026 with a number that product teams had been waiting two years to cite: 67% of B2B buyers now prefer to complete at least part of their purchase without ever speaking to a sales rep.

That statistic got shared across LinkedIn with captions like "the age of sales is over" and "PLG has officially won." It became the go-to slide for every product leader pitching a freemium motion to their board. And in one sense, those teams were not wrong. The buyer preference data is real, the trend is structural, and any SaaS company still requiring a discovery call before someone can try the product is fighting an uphill battle.

But the same month Gartner published that research, you could find an analysis from McKinsey that complicated the picture considerably. And most people sharing the Gartner slide had not read it.

## The Self-Serve Demand Is Real. The Revenue Often Is Not.

The buyer shift toward self-serve is not noise. [Gartner's March 2026 survey of 646 B2B buyers](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience) found that 67% prefer a rep-free experience for at least part of their purchase journey, up from 61% in a comparable 2025 survey. Forty-five percent said they used AI tools during a recent purchase. Forrester, meanwhile, issued a prediction that [more than half of large B2B transactions worth $1 million or more would flow through digital self-serve channels by end of 2025](https://investor.forrester.com/news-releases/news-release-details/forresters-b2b-marketing-sales-predictions-2025-more-half-large). That is not a SMB phenomenon. That is enterprise buyers choosing to route major contracts through a digital checkout rather than a sales process.

These numbers explain why product-led growth spread so fast across the SaaS market. In 2021, roughly 35% of SaaS companies identified as product-led, according to OpenView Partners tracking. By 2026 that share reached 60%. Every founder who watched Slack or Figma or Calendly grow without a traditional outbound sales force reasoned that they could replicate the motion. Many of them were right about the market demand and wrong about what it would take to capture it.

Adoption of a strategy and proficiency at it are different things. When you actually look at what companies running PLG strategy are generating in revenue relative to their sales-led peers, the narrative gets harder to sustain.

## What McKinsey Found When It Looked at 107 Public SaaS Companies

The analysis that rarely circulates in PLG communities is [McKinsey's examination of 107 publicly listed B2B SaaS companies](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/from-product-led-growth-to-product-led-sales-beyond-the-plg-hype). The headline finding was precise: most companies adopting product-led motions are not enjoying any revenue boost. On average, yes, PLG companies grow faster than sales-led peers. But the average is doing enormous work. A select subset of outperformers drives most of that disparity. Strip them out and the advantage largely disappears.

McKinsey's analysis also dismantled a closely held belief about PLG being inherently cheaper to run. High-performing product-led companies actually spend ten percentage points more on marketing, sales, and research and development combined than high-performing sales-led companies. The products that win with a self-serve motion are not cheaper to build or distribute. They simply generate enough distribution pull from the product itself that the higher absolute spend produces faster returns.

This is not an argument against product-led growth. It is an argument against the version of it that treats freemium as a distribution strategy and free tiers as a substitute for customer acquisition thought. The companies seeing no boost are, in McKinsey's framing, running a motion without an operating discipline behind it.

## The Measurement Gap That Explains Everything

Here is where the execution failure becomes specific and operational.

Ask the average PLG team what their activation rate is and you will encounter a spreadsheet from Q3, a definition that three people on the same team disagree about, or a blank look followed by a pivot to a different metric. Activation, in product-led terms, is the moment a new user experiences the product's core value for the first time: the aha moment, the first meaningful output, the point from which churn becomes substantially less likely. It is the behavioral threshold that separates users who eventually convert from users who disappear.

One widely cited industry figure puts the share of product-led companies that actively and consistently track activation at 34%. If accurate, that means roughly two-thirds of the companies calling themselves product-led are running an acquisition motion without the instrument that tells them whether it is working.

This is not a technical problem. Building activation tracking into a product is not hard. It is a prioritization problem, a signal that teams are optimizing for the visible activity (signup volume, feature clicks, onboarding completion) rather than the behavioral threshold that connects usage to revenue. Teams pour engineering capacity into tooltip sequences and email drips without knowing whether the users completing those flows are the ones who ever pay.

The measurement gap is why so many PLG dashboards look healthy while the conversion math stays broken.

The companies that close the measurement gap tend to land on the same structural answer: they stop running a pure self-serve motion and build a hybrid instead.

OpenView Partners and High Alpha tracked more than 800 SaaS companies in their [2024 SaaS Benchmarks report](https://www.highalpha.com/saas-benchmarks/2024) and found that 67% of hybrid PLG plus sales companies hit their net revenue retention targets, compared to 58% of pure product-led companies, a gap that held consistent across company sizes and market segments.

The mechanism is not complicated. Pure PLG leaves expansion to the product. Users upgrade when they hit seat limits or want premium features, and the motion works well for SMB accounts with bounded deal sizes. But when a SaaS company has accounts with $50,000 or $200,000 in potential expansion revenue, trusting an in-app upgrade prompt to capture that is a structural under-investment. Hybrid companies deploy a sales-assist layer precisely at the accounts where the product has already signaled buying intent, through usage thresholds, feature adoption patterns, or account growth signals. They capture expansion revenue that a pure product-led model leaves on the table.

This is also what McKinsey's data describes underneath the aggregate numbers. Most once-pure PLG companies that reached scale deployed their own sales teams. Atlassian added enterprise sales. Slack built an outbound function. Dropbox Business is now a sales-led product. The narrative that product replaces sales does not survive contact with the revenue data of the companies that supposedly proved it.

## Why Your SaaS Conversion Rate Is the Wrong Question

Most product-led teams measure free-to-paid conversion as their primary headline metric. It is the wrong place to look.

The median conversion rate across all freemium and free trial products sits around 9%, according to ChartMogul's SaaS Conversion Report. That aggregate number tells you almost nothing about whether your specific product is working, because it includes companies with broken onboarding, undefined activation events, and pricing structures that make self-serve upgrades economically irrational alongside companies that have all three working correctly.

The more predictive number is conversion rate by lead type. Companies routing product qualified leads, users who have already reached a defined behavioral threshold inside the product before being surfaced to sales, convert at 25% to 30%. Companies routing marketing qualified leads see 5% to 10%. The gap between those two rates is not a minor optimization. It is the difference between a self-sustaining growth loop and a CAC structure that requires continuous paid acquisition to stay afloat.

PQLs require activation tracking to exist. You cannot identify one without first defining what activated means for your product. Which is why the measurement gap and the conversion gap are, at their root, the same gap. Teams that do not know their activation rate cannot build a PQL threshold. Teams without a PQL threshold cannot separate high-intent free users from the users who signed up for the free tier and forgot the product exists.

This is the operational chain that connects the McKinsey finding to the daily reality of PLG execution.

## Time to Value Is the Lever Nobody Is Pulling

Gartner's rep-free preference data and Forrester's self-serve prediction point at the same operational requirement: buyers want to experience value before they commit to a contract. The expectation is not just that you have a free tier. It is that the free tier delivers something real, quickly enough that the user understands what they are paying for before they are asked to pay.

The benchmark that operationalizes this is time to value: the interval between signup and the moment a user reaches the product's core value promise. Best-in-class PLG companies target under 15 minutes for simple consumer tools and under 24 hours for complex B2B products. For developer-facing tools and API-first products, research consistently finds that extended delays in reaching the first meaningful output compound into significant conversion loss.

Where most product teams go wrong is designing onboarding to teach features rather than to deliver the activation event as fast as possible. Every screen, tooltip, mandatory profile field, and pre-onboarding survey step is a delay between signup and the moment that predicts whether someone pays. Winning PLG teams design the entire pre-activation experience as a funnel with one exit condition: reaching the activation event, or triggering a sales-assist workflow for accounts that need help getting there.

![Product-Led Growth Promised to Kill Sales Teams. The Winners Brought Them Back.](/blog/product-led-growth-promised-to-kill-sales-teams-the-winners-brought/infographic.svg)

The teams that design to that standard also discover something operationally useful: their product may not support self-serve activation for all buyer segments. Enterprise accounts often require a configuration conversation before the product can deliver value. Midmarket accounts may need a short implementation call. Recognizing precisely where your product enables fast, independent value delivery and where it does not is what makes the decision to layer in sales a legitimate architectural choice rather than an admission that the product is not good enough to sell itself.

## What PLG Winners Actually Do

The common thread across the data is that PLG winners do not choose between product and sales. They use the product to do three things with high efficiency: acquire users at scale without proportional CAC, qualify them behaviorally through defined activation events, and route the accounts with the highest expansion potential to a sales conversation at the right moment in the product journey.

That is what separates the select subset of high performers McKinsey identified from the majority seeing no revenue boost. They run acquisition through the product, qualification through usage data, and expansion through a targeted sales layer that only activates when the product has already done the qualifying work. The result is a hybrid GTM that combines the CAC efficiency of self-serve with the NRR of a sales-assisted expansion motion.

The companies that have not built this yet are not failing at the idea. They are failing at the measurement layer that makes PLG a real operating model rather than a positioning choice.

The 67% of buyers who prefer rep-free experiences are not saying they never want a human conversation. They are saying they want the product to earn that conversation first. The companies that figure this out will not replace their sales teams. They will build products that make those conversations worth having.
