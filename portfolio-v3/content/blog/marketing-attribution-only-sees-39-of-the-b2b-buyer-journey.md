*Why attribution only catches decisions already made*

![Industrial precision instrument panel with gauges and dials measuring an invisible process, dark metal background](/blog/marketing-attribution-only-sees-39-of-the-b2b-buyer-journey/hero.jpg)

Eighty-five percent of marketers say they can measure ROI, but according to Nielsen's 2025 Global Annual Marketing Survey, only [32 percent actually do it across all their channels](https://www.nielsen.com/news-center/2025/nielsen-unveils-makerting-roi-blueprint/). That 53-point gap is not a technology problem. It is a problem of what marketing attribution measurement is architecturally capable of seeing.

The same survey found that organizational alignment and clarity were marketers' top two challenges in measuring ROI. Not data access. Not tooling. Alignment. That is a signal about what attribution does to teams: it makes everyone fight about which channel gets credit for the same closed deal instead of asking whether the system is seeing the whole picture.

Here is what it is not seeing. According to [6sense's 2025 B2B Buyer Experience Report](https://6sense.com/b2b-buyer-experience-report/), buyers contact vendors for the first time at an average of 61 percent through their buying journey. That means the first 61 percent of every buying decision, the research, the peer conversations, the shortlist formation, happens without your CRM, your attribution software, or your marketing team knowing about it. Attribution only sees the final 39 percent of a decision that was already made.

## The gap between feeling measured and being measured

The confidence gap in Nielsen's data is worth sitting with for a moment. Eighty-five percent of marketers reporting high confidence in their ROI measurement while only 32 percent measure holistically is not a case of deluded professionals. It is a case of partial measurement feeling like complete measurement.

If you can see conversion paths in GA4, track source-attributed pipeline in Salesforce, and pull ROAS by channel from your ad platforms, you feel measured. And you are measured, within the slice of buyer behavior that shows up in those tools. The problem is that slice is the 39 percent that happens after a buyer has already decided they want to evaluate you.

The Nielsen data shows 60 percent of marketers are now incorporating both reach and frequency metrics alongside ROI into cross-media measurement strategies. That is an improvement. But it still misses the buying behavior that happens before anyone has seen your reach or clicked your ads.

The 6sense 2025 Buyer Experience Report surveyed more than 5,000 participants across major global markets. Its most consequential finding for anyone who works in B2B buying journey analytics is not the 61 percent journey completion figure. It is that [97 percent of buyers have prior personal experience with at least one vendor on their shortlist](https://6sense.com/b2b-buyer-experience-report/) before first contact.

The shortlist is not built during your nurture sequence. It is built from memory, from peer recommendations, from community posts, from podcast episodes, and from the kind of ambient brand presence that attribution classifies as "direct" or ignores entirely.

This is where the dark funnel concept becomes operationally important rather than theoretically interesting. The buying decision forms in Slack conversations between practitioners, in Reddit threads where someone asks for tool recommendations, in private LinkedIn DMs, and in AI search outputs that never produce a referral click your analytics can track. By the time a buyer fills out a form, they have usually eliminated everyone except two or three options. Attribution shows you the form fill. It does not show you the conversation that eliminated you or the content that kept you on the list.

## What attribution tracks and what it misses

Marketing attribution is measuring real things. Click paths, form submissions, email opens, ad impressions, and revenue attributed to those events are all genuine data points. The issue is that they represent the visible portion of a buying process that is structurally designed to be mostly invisible.

Consider what attribution cannot see even with perfect implementation. A buyer reads three of your LinkedIn posts over six months and remembers your company when their boss asks for vendor recommendations. A community manager on a Slack channel mentions your product favorably in response to a question. A podcast guest explains your approach to a problem the buyer is trying to solve. A buyer's colleague used your product at their previous company and trusts it. None of these appear in an attribution report.

When that buyer arrives at your website and converts, attribution says the last paid search click drove the conversion. Or the organic blog post. Or the LinkedIn ad they clicked two weeks prior. All of those are real touchpoints. None of them are the actual reason the buyer is there.

## Why your budget pressure and measurement gap compound each other

![Marketing attribution only sees 39% of the B2B buyer journey](/blog/marketing-attribution-only-sees-39-of-the-b2b-buyer-journey/infographic.svg)

Gartner's 2025 CMO Spend Survey found that [marketing budgets have flatlined at 7.7 percent of company revenue](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-05-12-gartner-2025-cmo-spend-survey-reveals-marketing-budgets-have-flatlined-at-seven-percent-of-overall-company-revenue), down from 9.5 percent just three years prior. Fifty-nine percent of CMOs report they have insufficient budget to execute their strategy. That flat budget number creates a specific kind of pressure: prove that every dollar you spend drives measurable pipeline, or lose it.

That pressure pushes marketing teams toward channels and tactics that attribution can measure. Because the board wants to see numbers, and attribution produces numbers, teams optimize for attribution-friendly activity. Performance marketing over brand. Retargeting over awareness. Last-touch channel optimization over influence-stage presence.

McKinsey's "Past Forward" research in 2025 found that [none of the 50-plus senior marketing leaders they interviewed could clearly articulate the ROI of their martech investments](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/past-forward-the-modern-rethinking-of-marketings-core). That is not a data scarcity problem. These organizations have more data than any marketing team in history. It is a measurement framing problem: they are measuring the last mile of a journey while making decisions about the full journey.

Improving marketing analytics and ROI measurement capability is expensive. And the budget pressure and the measurement gap create a feedback loop. Limited budget makes teams focus on what attribution can prove. What attribution can prove is the final 39 percent. The final 39 percent looks great in dashboards. So teams invest more in the final 39 percent and less in the 61 percent where the actual selection happens. Pipeline gets harder to generate. The team asks for more budget for the channels attribution credits. The board asks why the same channels are not performing as well as they used to. Nobody looks at whether the problem is upstream.

The IAB's State of Data 2026 report, surveying 400-plus senior decision-makers from US buy-side organizations, found that [60 to 75 percent of buy-side leaders say their core measurement approaches, including attribution analysis, incrementality tests, and marketing mix models, are underperforming](https://www.iab.com/state-of-data). This is not critics of the measurement industry. This is the measurement industry's own audience saying their tools are not adequate.

The same report found that AI-powered advanced measurement could release more than [$26 billion in media investment value](https://www.iab.com/state-of-data) over the next one to two years. The figure is an estimate, but the direction is unambiguous: the measurement gap is not a small operational inconvenience. It is a material financial problem at scale.

What makes this particularly pointed is that the 60 to 75 percent who say current approaches underperform are the people who have adopted all three major measurement approaches. These are not organizations using last-click attribution alone. These are organizations running incrementality tests, using marketing mix modeling, and operating multi-touch attribution simultaneously, and they are still saying the systems are not adequate.

This is what happens when your measurement stack is technically sophisticated but structurally blind to where decisions form. You can run the most rigorous incrementality test in the industry, and it will tell you whether a specific campaign drove incremental conversions in the visible 39 percent of the journey. It will not tell you whether your brand presence is strong enough in the influence stage to make it onto the shortlist at all.

## Three signals you need to track before the pipeline

The measurement gap is not fully closable. You cannot tag a private Slack conversation or instrument a podcast your buyer listened to while running. But you can build a richer picture of the influence stage by tracking proxies.

Branded search volume is the most reliable. When you run a campaign, podcast, or LinkedIn series and branded searches rise in the following weeks, you are seeing the influence stage responding. The buyer heard something, remembered your brand, and searched for it directly. Attribution will credit that conversion to "organic brand search," which is technically correct and strategically incomplete. The real signal is the volume change, not the channel.

Directly asking buyers how they heard about you, in a free-text field on your demo request form, produces data that consistently disagrees with what attribution says. In Refine Labs' research on 620 conversions, software attribution credited web search with 78 percent of sourced conversions, while buyer self-report credited dark social channels, word of mouth, and peer recommendations with the majority. The two data sources were measuring the same purchases and reaching opposite conclusions about what drove them.

Time-based brand lift studies, run against organic or paid campaigns, give you a third signal. When you pause campaign spend in a geographic holdout and measure the downstream difference in direct traffic, branded searches, and form conversions, you are creating an approximate view of the influence layer. It is not perfect, but it is more accurate than your current attribution stack about what brand presence is actually worth.

## Building presence where buying begins

The fix is not a better attribution model. Attribution will never see the first 61 percent of your buyer's journey, because that journey happens before buyers reveal themselves to you at all. Marketing ROI measurement that depends entirely on what attribution captures will always undercount the value of influence-stage investment. Attempting to instrument that phase with pixels and UTMs is like trying to measure a conversation that happened before anyone turned on the recording device.

What you can do is be present where those conversations happen. The peer communities where your buyers ask for recommendations. The podcasts where your potential buyers spend time. The LinkedIn content ecosystem where your buyers form views about which vendors are sophisticated. The Reddit threads where someone asks which tool handles a specific problem well. The owned content that is good enough to be cited in those conversations without you knowing.

None of this will show up cleanly in your attribution report. Some of it will show up as direct traffic. Some will show up as brand search. Some will show up as a sudden spike in inbound demand that your team will attribute to whatever campaign ran that month because that is what is in the data.

The CMOs who understand this do not stop measuring. They measure the 39 percent carefully with the tools available, run incrementality tests to validate what attribution says, use self-reported attribution to understand what attribution misses, and build their brand investment decisions on a combination of that evidence and judgment about where buying decisions actually form.

The buying decision isn't waiting for you to build a better attribution model; it's forming right now in a Slack thread, a Reddit post, and a podcast you've never appeared on, and the only way to show up there is to build the kind of consistent presence that attribution will credit to 'direct' and your team will call 'brand'.
