*Most SaaS companies are optimizing the metric that matters least.*

Most SaaS companies measure new ARR growth as their primary success metric, and most SaaS companies are leaving 40 to 67 percent of their growth potential sitting in accounts they already own.

This isn't an indictment of any individual team. It's a description of a structural lag that has become expensive. The go-to-market organizations built between 2018 and 2022 were designed for a specific growth environment: abundant capital, low interest rates, clear competitive differentiation, and a post-COVID demand surge that made new customer acquisition look easy and cheap. That environment is gone. The playbooks built for it are still running.

## The growth equation most SaaS companies are running is broken

[SaaS Capital's 2025 benchmark report](https://www.saas-capital.com/research/private-saas-company-growth-rate-benchmarks/), which tracks performance data across thousands of private software companies, found median growth rates for private B2B SaaS compressed from 47% in 2021 to 26% in 2025. That's not a temporary dip caused by a single macro event. It's a sustained multi-year compression that continued despite rate cuts, AI-driven efficiency gains, and renewed investor interest in the sector.

When growth compresses, the default organizational response is more of what already exists: more budget for demand generation, more SDRs, more outbound sequences, more optimization of the acquisition funnel. That's rational behavior given the incentive structures most teams operate inside. It's also the wrong response to what the data is actually showing.

The companies that maintained or grew their valuations through this compression period aren't the ones that figured out a better acquisition channel. They're the ones that figured out a better metric to optimize for. The distinction matters more than most leadership teams realize, because the metric shift requires a structural change in how you invest, measure, and compensate, not just a new campaign or a different ICP.

## What the valuation data actually says about NRR

McKinsey's [analysis of more than 100 B2B SaaS companies](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/the-net-revenue-retention-advantage-driving-success-in-b2b-tech), published in 2025 and covering data from Q1 2019 through Q4 2024, produced a finding that should reframe how every founder and growth leader thinks about their primary metric.

Companies in the top quartile of net revenue retention trade at a median enterprise value of 24 times revenue. Companies in the bottom quartile trade at 5 times revenue. That's a nearly five-fold gap in valuation multiple, driven primarily by a single metric that most SaaS finance reviews bury in a footnote.

The NRR threshold separating top from bottom quartile in McKinsey's dataset is narrower than most people expect. Top-quartile companies achieve 113% NRR. Bottom-quartile companies achieve 98%. A 15 percentage point difference in one metric creates a 4.8x difference in what the market believes your business is worth.

This finding held across six years of wildly different market conditions: the 2021 boom, the 2022 rate-driven correction, the 2023 and 2024 compression, and the early AI recovery cycle. The NRR premium wasn't a frothy-market artifact. It persisted through the full cycle.

Investors and acquirers are not primarily paying for revenue. They're paying for compounding revenue. A business with 113% NRR grows 13% next year even if it closes zero new customers. A business with 98% NRR needs to replace 2% of its customer base before it can call anything "growth." That difference in trajectory is worth 4.8x in enterprise value, consistently, across six years of market conditions, across funding environments that could not have looked more different from each other.

## Expansion ARR has quietly become the dominant growth engine

Here's what most SaaS dashboards aren't surfacing.

ChartMogul and Maxio's benchmark data, corroborated by the Growth Unhinged [2025 SaaS Benchmarks Report](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/2025-saas-benchmarks-report) drawing from more than 800 companies, shows that expansion revenue (additional ARR from upsells, seat growth, usage expansion, and cross-sell within existing accounts) rose from roughly 25% of total new ARR in 2022 to 40% in 2024. That's a 60% shift in where growth comes from over two years, with no corresponding shift in how most GTM teams are organized or measured.

The trend intensifies at scale. For companies above $50M ARR, expansion now accounts for 58 to 67% of all new revenue. Majority growth is coming from accounts that your sales team already closed, not from new logos or outbound campaigns.

This shift isn't primarily happening because companies are running sophisticated upsell programs. A lot of it is structural: seat expansion as customer organizations grow, usage expansion in consumption-priced products, cross-sell into adjacent modules as product lines mature. The expansion is happening with relatively little deliberate investment from most GTM teams.

[Recurly's 2025 Churn Report](https://recurly.com/resources/the-churn-report) puts median monthly B2B SaaS churn at 3.5%, broken into 2.6% voluntary cancellations and 0.8% from billing failures. On the expansion side, the economics look fundamentally different from new logo acquisition: industry benchmark data consistently shows that generating a dollar of expansion ARR costs roughly $0.20 to $0.27 in sales and marketing spend, versus $1.16 for a dollar of new logo ARR. Your existing customer base is your cheapest growth channel. It's also your least-measured, least-invested, and least-compensated one.

## Why most SaaS GTM teams are organized for the wrong problem

The structural issue isn't that SaaS leaders don't understand expansion is valuable. Most do, at least intellectually. The issue is that the SaaS growth strategy most organizations actually execute still optimizes for acquisition at every lever that matters: compensation, attribution, budget allocation, and board-level reporting.

Sales compensation plans pay full quota credit for new logos and discounted or zero credit for expansion. Marketing pipelines are measured by new MQL volume, not account engagement scores or product usage signals that predict expansion readiness. Board metrics show new ARR growth and logo count as headline numbers, with NRR relegated to a supporting appendix. Demand gen budgets skew heavily toward top-of-funnel campaigns targeting new buyers rather than programs designed to accelerate growth in existing accounts.

This isn't the result of bad strategic thinking. It's the result of incentive structures built during a period when acquisition was the only lever that moved fast enough to satisfy investor growth expectations. Those structures worked for the environment they were designed for. They've created institutional resistance to the pivot that the current environment requires.

The consequence is that teams are making individually rational decisions that collectively misallocate capital relative to where the highest return actually sits. Every SDR hired to prospect new accounts instead of develop existing ones represents a marginal dollar invested in a $1.16-cost channel when a $0.25-cost channel sits largely untapped next door.

## What 113% NRR actually requires

Getting to 113% NRR, the threshold that separates top-quartile companies in McKinsey's dataset, is not primarily a product problem. It's an operational and organizational problem.

The [same McKinsey research](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/the-net-revenue-retention-advantage-driving-success-in-b2b-tech) found that companies with sophisticated customer value-realization programs achieve NRR roughly 7 percentage points higher than peers running basic onboarding. For a company at $30M ARR, 7 additional NRR percentage points compounds to tens of millions in enterprise value over five years. The gap between "basic onboarding" and "sophisticated value-realization" is an operational question, not a product engineering question.

What separates sophisticated from basic in practice? Three things consistently appear in the operating models of high-NRR companies.

### Usage-correlated expansion triggers

High-NRR companies don't schedule expansion conversations on the calendar. They build signals that surface when accounts show behavioral readiness to grow: hitting 80% of a usage limit, spreading adoption to a second internal team, or showing the engagement patterns that historically precede upgrades. The expansion conversation happens when the customer's behavior shows they're already finding value, not when it's convenient for the sales calendar. The accounts most ready to expand are often the quietest ones on your CRM because they're busy using the product.

### Expansion mechanics built into the product

The highest-ROI expansion motion is in-product, not in-person. In-app prompts at natural upgrade moments, usage dashboards that surface the case for higher tiers, seat recommendations generated from adoption patterns. Companies running above 120% NRR almost always have at least one of these mechanics actively driving upgrade behavior. The best ones have three, sequenced across the customer lifecycle so expansion pressure exists at every stage of adoption, not just at renewal.

### Customer success as a revenue function

The most important organizational shift high-NRR companies have made is treating customer success as a direct revenue team, with a quota on expansion ARR, ownership of NRR as a primary metric, and the tooling and data to hit it. This is not the same as making CS responsible for retention. It's making CS accountable for growth within accounts, which requires different data access, different conversation training, and a different relationship with both the product team and finance.

None of these ideas are new. The execution gap is wide specifically because the acquisition side of the business has always been louder, faster-moving, and better-resourced. Building organizational commitment to expansion operations takes deliberate effort against institutional gravity that points toward new logo acquisition.

## The PLG conversion paradox

Product-led growth is often framed as the model that naturally solves the expansion problem, letting the product drive adoption until revenue follows automatically.

The reality is more layered. [OpenView's SaaS benchmark data](https://openviewpartners.com/2023-saas-benchmarks-report/) found that only 5% of freemium signups convert from free to paid, yet product-led companies grow roughly twice as fast as sales-led peers over time. Both statistics are true. Reading them together is instructive.

The 2x growth advantage of PLG companies doesn't come primarily from better free-to-paid conversion rates. It comes from expansion within accounts that have already converted. PLG creates a natural surface for in-product upsell through usage-based pricing, seat growth mechanics, and feature tier progression. But activating that surface still requires deliberate effort: pricing architecture that rewards growth, product instrumentation that surfaces expansion opportunities, and a CS function that follows product signals and acts on them before accounts plateau.

Running a PLG motion and having an expansion problem are not mutually exclusive, and many companies discover this the hard way. The companies that have resolved the tension treat product-led expansion within existing accounts as a distinct operation from product-led acquisition of new users. The data on expansion ARR shows these are different mechanisms with different drivers, and most PLG frameworks conflate them in ways that leave significant revenue uncaptured.

## Three changes SaaS growth teams should make now

Reorienting your SaaS growth strategy around expansion rather than acquisition is not a one-quarter project. But most teams can take the first meaningful steps within the next 90 days, before the next board review and before the next headcount planning cycle locks in another year of acquisition-first investment.

**Add expansion ARR as a first-class board metric.** If your board deck shows new logo ARR and total ARR but doesn't break out expansion ARR separately, you're measuring growth without measuring your most efficient growth channel. Carve it out as a separate line in the board deck. Assign clear ownership to a named individual. Review it monthly with the same rigor you apply to pipeline. The metric you don't report is the metric your organization doesn't optimize, because no one is compensated to care about it.

**Restructure at least one quota-carrying role around expansion.** Whether that means a dedicated expansion account executive, a customer success team with explicit ARR responsibility, or a hybrid model where existing AEs own both retention and growth within their accounts, pick a structure and commit to it for at least two quarters. The expansion signal needs a human following it with a financial stake in the outcome. Good intentions without quota don't move the needle.

**Build one expansion trigger in your product before the end of the year.** This doesn't require a product team reorg or a six-month roadmap negotiation. It requires identifying the behavioral signal that most reliably predicts expansion readiness in your existing accounts, typically hitting a usage threshold or spreading adoption to a second internal team, and building one in-product message that fires when that signal appears. A single trigger, properly instrumented and followed up on, often generates more expansion ARR in a quarter than a full outbound upsell campaign running all year.

The SaaS companies that win the next five years won't be the ones who close the most new logos. They'll be the ones who figured out that the most valuable customer is the one already inside the tent.
