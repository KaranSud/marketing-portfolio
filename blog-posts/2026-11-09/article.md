# B2B SaaS Marketing Tactics Miss 92% of Decision-Makers

*The case against marketing to one person in a room of 13*

[HERO IMAGE HERE]

Every B2B SaaS purchase decision in 2026 involves, on average, 13 internal stakeholders and 9 external influencers (a room that most marketing funnels were never designed to enter).

That figure comes from [Forrester's 2026 State of Business Buying report](https://www.forrester.com/press-newsroom/forrester-2026-the-state-of-business-buying/), which surveyed nearly 18,000 global business buyers across industries and company sizes. It's the most comprehensive look at B2B purchase dynamics published this year, and its headline number is uncomfortable for most SaaS marketing teams.

If your marketing strategy still centers on a single buyer persona, a gated content offer, an MQL threshold, and a sales handoff, you're influencing, at most, 1 of 13 people before the deal enters your pipeline. The other 12 are forming opinions elsewhere, in private Slack channels, analyst calls, peer conversations, vendor comparison threads, and AI research queries you have no visibility into.

That's the 92% problem. It's not a tactical issue you can fix with better targeting or smarter sequencing. It's structural, and the companies widening the revenue gap already know it.

## The Room You're Not Marketing To

Forrester surveyed [nearly 18,000 global business buyers](https://www.forrester.com/press-newsroom/forrester-2026-the-state-of-business-buying/) to track how B2B purchase decisions form in practice. The 2026 edition documented a committee that spans the economic buyer, the champion, the technical evaluator, procurement, legal, security, IT operations, and however many end-user representatives weigh in on a platform decision.

The 9 external influencers are worth pausing on. These are not people your CRM will ever show you. They include independent consultants advising the buying organization, industry analysts whose opinions circulate in committee discussions, peers at other companies who've already implemented your product category, and the reference customers your champion calls without telling you. Each of them shapes the outcome of your deal before you receive a single form submission.

Procurement professionals now participate in 53% of buying cycles, up from prior years. That's a function with explicit veto power appearing in more than half of all deals. Most SaaS marketing teams have produced exactly zero content designed to give a procurement officer what they need to get comfortable approving the spend. No total cost of ownership documentation. No vendor risk assessment framework. No comparison criteria that speak to their actual job, which is not to validate the champion's enthusiasm but to protect the organization from a bad contract.

The math is straightforward. A marketing program built around a single persona is producing influence over roughly 1 in 13 people whose opinion affects the outcome. When a deal stalls in legal review or dies in procurement, it's rarely because the champion changed their mind about the product. It's because the other 12 people never got what they needed to feel confident.

## How Single-Persona Marketing Became the Default

This architecture made sense at a smaller scale. In 2010, the average B2B buying committee hovered around 5 to 6 people, centered on a single economic buyer and a couple of technical evaluators. The demand generation playbook that emerged from that era (build a persona, create gated content for that persona, capture an MQL, hand it to sales) was reasonably fit for purpose then.

The CEB research that became the foundation of the *Challenger Customer* (2015) first documented committee expansion, and the numbers have climbed since. Committees grew larger as software became more deeply integrated, more expensive, and more consequential to business operations. A $20,000 ACV point solution one team could buy without broad approval became a $200,000 ACV platform that required sign-off from six departments and triggered a procurement review.

But most SaaS marketing teams didn't update the architecture alongside that change. They updated the tactics (better ABM software, more sophisticated intent data signals, sharper personalization at the champion level) while keeping the same conceptual unit: one persona, one content track, one MQL threshold, one handoff moment. The room got larger and more complicated, and the marketing program stayed the same size.

This is the single-persona fallacy. Not that personas are wrong as a concept, but that treating one persona as the sufficient unit of a B2B SaaS marketing program systematically produces blindness to the other 12 rooms where your deal is being shaped.

## 67% Through the Door Before You Know They Exist

The committee problem compounds with a timing problem.

[Gartner's March 2026 survey of 646 B2B buyers](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience) found that 67% of B2B buyers prefer a rep-free purchase experience. Among those buyers, 45% used AI as part of their research process during their most recent purchase. That preference isn't about wanting an inferior experience. It's about wanting to form a view before anyone with a quota gets involved in the conversation.

What this produces, operationally, is a buying journey that completes a substantial portion of itself before any vendor knows it's happening. [6sense's 2024 Buyer Experience Report](https://6sense.com/resource/2024-b2b-buyer-experience-report/), based on surveys of approximately 4,000 buyers, found that buyers are 61% through a 10.1-month buying journey before they contact a vendor for the first time.

Work through what that means concretely. For a typical $100,000 ACV SaaS deal, the decisive 6 months of shortlist formation and committee alignment happens entirely in channels you don't have insight into. The 13 stakeholders are discussing your product category, comparing vendors against each other, sharing articles their peers recommended, checking your G2 reviews, and forming opinions about whether your company feels credible, all before a single contact enters your CRM.

Your first MQL is not the beginning of the buyer's journey. It lands approximately two-thirds of the way through it. And the 11 other stakeholders who haven't filled out a form yet are still forming their views.

The B2B SaaS marketing tactics that optimize purely for lead capture are, structurally, optimizing for the final third of a 10-month process. That's not demand generation. It's demand capture, from buyers who've already largely decided.

## The Revenue Consequence Is Already Showing

This isn't an abstract positioning problem. [McKinsey's 2026 Global B2B Pulse Survey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-surprising-economics-of-b2b-growth-the-new-survival-threshold-and-what-it-takes-to-thrive), which drew on nearly 4,000 B2B decision-makers across 13 countries, found that 60% of market leaders reported double-digit revenue growth, compared with 21% of laggards. Leaders were defined as companies growing market share by more than 10%.

The differentiating behaviors the report documents cluster around buyer-centricity and digital breadth: offering self-service options across the committee's research process, being findable in the channels buyers use before vendor contact, aligning commercial motions to how groups evaluate rather than how individual contacts move through a funnel.

This is not about spending more. McKinsey's data shows the gap between leaders and laggards isn't primarily a budget gap. It's an architectural gap. The companies generating 3 times more double-digit revenue growth are marketing to the room, not just to the contact record.

The implication for SaaS marketing teams is uncomfortable but clear. If you're optimizing a single-persona MQL funnel while your highest-growth competitors are building committee-level visibility across 10-month dark journeys, you're not competing on the same plane. You're playing a different and slower game, and the pipeline efficiency numbers will eventually reflect it.

## Where the Budget Signal Points

[Gartner's 2026 CMO Spend Survey](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-11-gartner-2026-cmo-spend-survey-finds-cmos-allocate-15-point-3-percent-of-marketing-budgets-to-ai-but-only-30-percent-are-ready-to-scale-ai-capabilities) covered 401 marketing leaders across North America, the UK, and Europe, surveyed from January through March 2026. Marketing budgets average 7.8% of company revenue. At a $100M ARR SaaS company, that's $7.8 million. Of that, 15.3% (roughly $1.19 million) is now being allocated to AI marketing tools and initiatives.

The problem: 70% of those same CMOs report that their internal marketing processes are not mature enough to scale their AI investments. And 56% say their marketing organization lacks the budget required to deliver their 2026 strategy.

What's happening is an allocation problem disguised as a budget problem. Teams are spending more on AI tools, most of which accelerate content production and personalization within single-persona funnels. The structural mismatch (1 persona, 13 stakeholders, 10-month dark journey) stays unchanged. You cannot automate your way out of marketing to the wrong unit.

The irony of the AI marketing budget surge is that most of it is making the wrong architecture faster. Faster persona content. Faster email personalization. Faster MQL scoring. None of it addresses the committee members who never trigger a marketing touchpoint because they're doing their research in channels that don't connect to your stack.

## Three Structural Fixes for the 13-Person Problem

Solving the 92% problem requires architectural shifts, not new tools layered on top of the same structure.

**Expand the content unit from persona to committee role.** A procurement officer evaluating your product needs different information than your champion. So does the CISO, the CFO, and the IT lead who'll own the integration. Each committee role carries predictable objections, information needs, and trust signals. Building content specifically for procurement (ROI calculators, vendor risk frameworks, security certification documentation, total cost of ownership models) is not extra workload. It's the minimum viable marketing for a 13-person decision. Without it, your champion is trying to sell internally using assets built for external consumption, and the conversation stalls.

**Invest in channels where committee members research before contacting you.** This is where dark funnel B2B strategy stops being jargon and becomes operational: G2 and Capterra review management so the independent researcher finds credible third-party validation; LinkedIn organic content your champion can forward to a skeptical CFO without it looking like a sales pitch; analyst brief programs for the external influencers who advise the committee without appearing in your CRM; and presence in the peer communities where practitioners build category opinions over months. These channels don't generate direct MQL attribution. They generate the conditions under which the champion can close the committee.

**Change the measurement unit from MQL to account activation.** An MQL tells you one person crossed an engagement threshold. Account activation tells you that multiple roles within a target account are showing engagement signals across channels simultaneously (the champion downloaded the ROI guide, two end-user IDs visited the integration documentation, the company appeared in a G2 category search). The concept of marketing-qualified accounts (MQAs) captures this better than leads: an account crosses the threshold when multiple committee roles have been influenced, not when one contact fills a form. That pattern predicts pipeline velocity better than any single-contact threshold, and it gives sales a materially different briefing about how much buying committee marketing work has already been done before the discovery call.

None of these fixes require a larger budget. What they require is rethinking the fundamental unit that B2B SaaS marketing tactics are built around, from the individual lead to the buying committee.

## The 2030 Case for Acting Now

Here's the forward-looking context that makes this investment logic feel durable rather than reactive.

[Gartner's August 2025 research](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-08-25-gartner-says-by-2030-that-75-percent-of-b2b-buyers-will-prefer-sales-experiences-that-prioritize-human-interaction-over-ai) predicts that by 2030, 75% of B2B buyers will prefer sales experiences that prioritize human interaction over AI. Right now, 67% prefer rep-free. That preference is set to invert over the next four years.

The driver of that inversion is fatigue. When every vendor's research experience is AI-generated, undifferentiated, and optimized for a single persona type, buyers will tire of it and return to wanting human judgment at the decisive moments of a high-stakes purchase. The peer recommendation from someone who's been through implementation. The analyst who knows the category's weaknesses. The human on the other side of a conversation who can read the room and adapt.

The companies that will own the 2030 B2B SaaS market are not waiting for that moment to build their authority. They're building now: committee-level relationships, analyst credibility, community presence, and the kind of peer reputation that the 9 external influencers Forrester counted carry into every buying discussion they're part of. These things compound slowly. They cannot be turned on in a quarter when demand drops or a competitor captures your category's search results.

The companies building committee-level authority in the channels buyers use before they ever reach out are setting up not just for next quarter, but for a 2030 market where the same buyers who've been researching silently finally decide they want to talk to a human.
