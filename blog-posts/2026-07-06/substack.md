# Marketing Attribution Created the Doom Loop. Here's How to Break It.

*84% of companies are stuck. Last-touch attribution put them there.*

Flip open any board deck that includes a marketing update and you'll find the same uncomfortable silence: a chart that shows spend going up, a chart that shows conversions holding flat, and a CMO who can't explain the gap.

That silence has a name now. Gartner calls it the "brand doom loop," and as of June 2026, [84% of companies are living inside it](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-06-10-gartner-marketing-survey-finds-84-of-companies-are-stuck-in-a-brand-doom-loop). The loop works like this: marketing can't prove its value clearly enough to justify more budget, so it gets less budget, which makes measurement even harder, which makes proving value even more impossible. The cycle keeps feeding itself.

What nobody is saying out loud is that marketing attribution (specifically the last-touch variant that most companies still use as their default) is what built the loop in the first place.

## Half of All CMOs Can't Prove Their Department's Value

The scale of this problem is worth sitting with for a moment. In a [Gartner survey of 378 senior marketing leaders](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-09-18-gartner-survey-finds-only-52-of-senior-marketing-leaders-can-prove-marketings-value-and-receive-credit-for-its-contribution-to-business-outcomes), conducted in spring 2024, only 52% reported successfully proving the value of marketing and receiving credit for contributions to business outcomes. That means roughly half of the people running major marketing organizations cannot make a credible case that their work is doing anything.

Think about what that means in practice. These are not junior marketers. These are CMOs and senior marketing leaders at companies large enough to pay for Gartner research subscriptions. They have dashboards, attribution software, and analytics teams. And still, half of them walk out of budget meetings unable to defend what they spent.

The obvious question is: how does this happen? The answer starts with CMO ROI measurement systems that were built to answer a question nobody is actually asking.

The question attribution software answers: "Which channel recorded the last interaction before a conversion?"

The question the CFO is asking: "Did your marketing spending cause revenue to grow?"

These are not the same question. The tools are measuring something real. They're just measuring the wrong thing.

## The Doom Loop Is a System, Not a Symptom

Most commentary on the brand doom loop treats it as a consequence of budget cuts, or executive skepticism, or the general difficulty of proving brand value. All of that is true. What gets skipped is the mechanism: why does the loop sustain itself, and why does it keep getting worse?

Gartner's June 2026 research on [the doom loop](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-06-10-gartner-marketing-survey-finds-84-of-companies-are-stuck-in-a-brand-doom-loop) found that companies inside it are half as likely to exceed their organizational growth targets as those that can evaluate brand value clearly. The loop doesn't just feel bad. It actively kills performance. Companies stuck in the loop underinvest, underperform, then conclude that marketing doesn't work and cut further.

The self-reinforcing logic runs like this. When you can't measure the value of brand-building and upper-funnel investment, you pull budget from it. You shift that budget toward channels where conversions are easy to track: branded search, retargeting, performance display. Those channels show strong attribution numbers. The CFO sees the numbers. The budget for trackable channels stays safe. The brand channels atrophy.

Then a few quarters later, the performance channels start getting more expensive, because they're capturing demand that already existed rather than creating new demand. Efficiency declines, and the CFO asks for the ROI story again. The CMO doesn't have one, and the budget gets trimmed.

That is the loop. The measurement system that directed budget toward attributable channels and away from brand is still running in the background, still producing the same distorted picture, still making the same recommendations.

## Last-Touch Attribution Built the Loop

The clearest signal that attribution pressure is reshaping where marketing money goes comes from Gartner's [June 2026 CMO Spend Survey](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-06-08-gartner-marketing-survey-finds-awareness-and-conversion-account-for-62-6-of-total-media-spend) of 401 CMOs. Awareness and conversion spending now accounts for 62.6% of total media budgets, up more than 10% since 2024. Loyalty and retention spending dropped 29% over the same period and now sits below 15% of total media spend.

> **[CHART 2]** Take a screenshot of chart2.html and upload it here. Title: "Where CMOs Put Their Media Budget: Acquisition vs. Loyalty (2022–2026)." Caption: "Source: Gartner 2026 CMO Spend Survey, 401 CMOs."

The story in those numbers is not that CMOs are bad at strategy. The story is that their measurement systems are telling them to do this. When last-touch attribution assigns the majority of conversion credit to the final click before a purchase (typically branded search, retargeting, or direct), those channels look like the ones that drive revenue. They get more budget. Channels that operate earlier in the journey, the ones that create awareness and build preference before a prospect ever searches your brand name, look like poor performers and get defunded.

Over a long enough timeline, this creates a portfolio that is very good at capturing demand that already exists and very bad at creating new demand. The pipeline starts thinning. The CFO wants answers. The CMO doesn't have good ones, because the measurement system that drove the strategy also made it impossible to explain what happened.

This is not a new observation. Les Binet, who with Peter Field produced decades of IPA effectiveness research, spent years documenting what happens when companies over-rotate toward performance marketing and short-term attribution. The trend accelerated during the era of cheap digital clicks, and now it's visible in the budget data.

## Why Buying Better Attribution Tools Doesn't Help

The industry response to measurement failure has been, almost uniformly, to buy more attribution software: multi-touch attribution platforms, customer data platforms, revenue attribution tools, attribution modelers that use machine learning to distribute credit across more touchpoints.

None of this addresses the underlying problem, which is that [64% of B2B marketing leaders don't trust their marketing measurement](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/b2b-marketing-leaders-dont-trust-their-measurement-and-what-they-measure-isnt-helping/) for decision-making, according to Forrester's 2024 Marketing Survey. Not "don't have access to measurement." They have dashboards, they have the software, they've read the reports. They just don't believe the numbers are telling them the truth.

More attribution tooling doesn't solve a trust problem. It often deepens it, because additional complexity introduces new opportunities for the numbers to diverge from reality in ways that are harder to explain.

The fundamental issue with marketing attribution as a philosophy is that it tries to assign credit deterministically to a process that is inherently probabilistic. A buyer researches your product over six months, talks to three colleagues who read your newsletter, attends a webinar, googles your competitor, hears about you from a customer at a conference, then clicks a retargeting ad and converts. Your attribution system credits the ad. Your attribution system is not lying. It is correctly describing what happened last. It is wrong about what caused the conversion.

Pranav Piyush, CEO of Paramark (the measurement startup that raised $8 million in 2025), has been blunt about this: "Ninety percent of attribution tech is theater." The dashboards are real. The data pipeline is real. The insight about what caused revenue growth is, in most cases, not there.

## The $26 Billion Case for Rebuilding from First Principles

There is an honest accounting for what bad measurement costs. The [IAB's State of Data 2026](https://www.iab.com/insights/2026-state-of-data-report/) report, based on a survey of more than 400 senior planning and analytics decision-makers, estimated that improvements in measurement could release $26.3 billion in media investment value and $6.2 billion in industry-wide productivity, within one to two years. That figure represents the waste baked into current measurement systems: budget deployed to channels that look effective under attribution but are not creating incremental growth, and budget pulled from channels that create growth but can't prove it.

The path out of the doom loop is not another attribution model. It is a measurement philosophy that can answer the causal question: not "which channel appeared at the end?" but "did this investment cause revenue to grow that would not have grown otherwise?"

That philosophy has three components that can be implemented without a full martech overhaul.

Self-reported attribution is the easiest and most underused. Add a single question to every pipeline intake form, every post-purchase survey, and every demo request: "How did you first hear about us?" The answer will not match what your CRM shows as the source. That gap (between what customers say influenced them and what your software credits) is the measurement gap. Companies like Refine Labs have published data showing that software-based attribution misses 93% of LinkedIn-influenced pipeline because LinkedIn operates at the beginning of buying journeys, not the end.

Incrementality testing turns the question causal. Rather than asking "which channel gets credit," you ask "what would have happened if we hadn't run this?" A geo-holdout test, run for four to six weeks in a single channel, compares regions where you ran the campaign against regions where you didn't. The revenue difference is the actual incremental impact, not a modeled approximation of it. This is how Google and Meta validate their own ad products internally.

Marketing mix modeling (the statistical approach that predates digital attribution by decades) has made a significant comeback as privacy restrictions have eroded click-level tracking. Google and Meta both open-sourced their MMM tools in 2023 and 2024, a signal that the industry is acknowledging that individual-level attribution is no longer reliable at scale. MMM operates at the aggregate level, using historical spend and revenue data to model the contribution of each channel over time. It cannot tell you which individual buyer responded to which ad. It can tell you whether your brand spend is producing revenue growth, which is the question the CFO is actually asking.

The three approaches work together. Self-reported attribution gives you directional signal on what influenced buyers. Incrementality testing gives you causal proof for specific channels and campaigns. MMM gives you the portfolio-level view for budget allocation. None of them require perfect data or unlimited budget. All of them give you answers that hold up in a board meeting.

## What the Next 18 Months Look Like for CMOs Who Don't Fix This

Gartner's [February 2026 prediction](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-2-12-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-cmos-who-push-for-larger-brand-budgets-will-lose-influence-with-the-c-suite) is unusually specific: by 2027, over 40% of CMOs who push for larger brand budgets will lose influence with the C-suite because they cannot demonstrate sufficient returns. That is a career consequence attached to a measurement failure. Not a prediction that brand spend will be cut (it already is). A prediction that the CMOs who advocate for it without the proof infrastructure to back their case will personally lose standing.

The companies in the doom loop are already half as likely to exceed their growth targets. The CMOs leading them are, per Gartner's research, at real risk of losing C-suite credibility by the end of 2027. The thing that would change both outcomes is not a better attribution vendor. It is a change in what the measurement system is designed to prove.

## Three Moves to Break the Loop

The loop is solvable. Here is what the first 90 days looks like for a team that decides to break it.

Week one: add a "how did you first hear about us" field to every lead form and demo request. You do not need IT. You do not need a new platform. You need one field and someone who will look at the answers. Start comparing the answers to your CRM source data. The divergence will be immediately visible.

Weeks four through ten: run a geo-holdout experiment in one channel. Pick a paid social platform or a content syndication program. Split your geography into test and control regions. Pause the campaign in control regions. Run it normally in test. Measure the revenue difference after six weeks. That number is the incremental value of the channel, verified by comparison rather than modeled by software.

Month three: bring marketing mix modeling into the budget conversation. You do not need to build it yourself. Paramark, Meridian (Google's open-source tool), and Robyn (Meta's open-source tool) are all available. Run a basic model using the last two years of spend and revenue data. Use the output to show the CFO which channels have contributed to growth at the portfolio level. That is a conversation most CMOs have never been able to have.

None of this is fast. Measurement is a slow-build capability, and the doom loop took years to construct. But each of these moves gives you a more defensible answer to the question the C-suite is asking, and each one makes the next budget conversation less uncomfortable than the last.

The loop breaks the moment you stop asking which channel closed the deal and start asking which investments created the demand, and once you see the difference, you cannot unsee it.
