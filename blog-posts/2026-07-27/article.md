Most SaaS marketing teams are optimizing for the growth motion that made 2020 famous, and it's quietly killing their valuations.

I'm not talking about a gradual drift. This is a structural misalignment between where SaaS value is actually being created in 2026 and where most marketing and sales budgets are still being deployed. Global software spending [hit $1.44 trillion this year, growing 14.7% year over year](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-02-03-gartner-forecasts-worldwide-it-spending-to-grow-10-point-8-percent-in-2026-totaling-6-point-15-trillion-dollars) according to Gartner, so the market is not contracting. But the mechanism for winning inside that market has shifted, and the standard SaaS growth strategy is still running playbooks written for a different era.

The companies pulling away from the pack are not doing it by building better demand generation funnels or finding cheaper lead acquisition channels. They are doing it by systematically mastering a discipline that most marketing teams still treat as someone else's job: growing the revenue they already have.

## What a 15-Point NRR Gap Actually Costs You

NRR is not a customer success metric. It is a valuation metric dressed in customer success clothing, and the gap between who gets that and who doesn't is widening fast.

McKinsey published research in late 2025 that I keep returning to. Surveying more than 100 commercial and revenue leaders across 98 B2B SaaS companies, they found something stark: [top-quartile SaaS companies achieve 113% net revenue retention while bottom-quartile companies manage 98%](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/the-net-revenue-retention-advantage-driving-success-in-b2b-tech). That 15-point gap corresponds to a median enterprise-value-to-revenue multiple of 24x for the top quartile and 5x for the bottom.

Fifteen percentage points separating a 24x company from a 5x one.

If you run a $20 million ARR business and your NRR is 113% instead of 98%, you are not looking at a better retention number. You are looking at a categorically different outcome conversation when you talk to investors or run an exit process. And this gap has been stable across the market cycle. It is not a 2021 artifact.

[CHART 1 HERE]

The reason it is so durable is that NRR operates as a compounding mechanism. A company at 113% NRR that adds zero net new customers still grows 13% year over year from its existing accounts. A company at 98% NRR is shrinking with the same acquisition budget working against it. Over a five-year horizon, those two businesses do not look like variations of the same thing. They are unrecognizable from each other.

The broader NRR trend makes this urgent. After the expansion era of 2020 and 2021, median SaaS NRR peaked near 105%. It has compressed consistently since, sitting around 101% across private SaaS companies as of 2024. The companies holding above 110% are not running on luck. They have built deliberate, funded expansion motions. The ones hovering at 98% are mostly relying on new logo acquisition to paper over what their retention is losing.

## The Math That Should Embarrass Every Acquisition-First Team

The argument here is purely financial, and it is cleaner than most acquisition-versus-expansion debates get.

According to [Benchmarkit's 2025 SaaS performance metrics](https://www.benchmarkit.ai/2025benchmarks), the median SaaS company spends $2.00 in sales and marketing to acquire every $1.00 of new customer ARR. That is the new customer acquisition cost ratio, measured across 342 SaaS and AI-native software companies. The median B2B company recovers that cost in 16 months, with top-quartile companies doing it in 6 months or fewer and bottom-quartile companies taking 24 months or longer.

None of those numbers touch expansion. They capture only the cost of landing a new customer.

Expansion revenue, by contrast, whether it comes from upsells, seat additions, usage overages, or cross-sells into adjacent products, costs a fraction of that figure. The directional research across multiple benchmarking sources consistently puts expansion ARR acquisition at roughly 15 to 25 cents for every dollar of expanded revenue, versus over a dollar for a net-new customer. That efficiency ratio is not marginal. It is the difference between a profitable growth motion and an expensive one.

And yet the typical SaaS marketing team spends the majority of its budget on demand generation aimed at new logos. Content programs, paid search, outbound sequences, events, all pointed at the top of the funnel. The post-sale lifecycle, where the most efficient revenue in your business is waiting, gets whatever is left over.

## The Tipping Point Most Finance Teams See, But Marketing Misses

The 40% figure is where the conversation starts to get uncomfortable for acquisition-first teams.

According to the [2024 SaaS Benchmarks Report from High Alpha and OpenView](https://www.highalpha.com/saas-benchmarks/2024), median SaaS companies now generate 40% of their new ARR from expansion. Companies that cross $50 million in ARR hit a structural tipping point: expansion ARR exceeds new logo ARR. More than half of their growth is coming from customers they already have, not customers they are still trying to find.

[CHART 2 HERE]

This is not a plateau. Expansion as a share of new ARR has been growing steadily, up from roughly 25% in 2022 to 40% at the median today. What drove the shift? A combination of forces landed at roughly the same time. CAC climbed as competition intensified and paid channels saturated. Buyers became more cautious and stretched their sales cycles. And the companies that had invested in customer success infrastructure and expansion programs years earlier started seeing those investments compound in their NRR numbers.

The result is that your finance team, if they are running cohort analysis on ARR, already knows this. They are watching expansion ARR carry more of the growth number every quarter. The question is whether your marketing organization has reorganized around that reality or is still treating post-sale revenue as somebody else's job.

## The Self-Service Paradox

Here is where it gets genuinely interesting, because the data points in two directions at once.

[A March 2026 Gartner survey of 646 B2B buyers](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience) found that 67% of B2B buyers now prefer a rep-free buying experience. Two-thirds of your potential customers would rather self-serve through your product than talk to a salesperson through any part of the purchase process.

The obvious read on this data is: build better PLG. Make the product sell itself. Reduce friction everywhere in the acquisition funnel. Invest in self-serve onboarding and in-app conversion.

That read is incomplete, and in practice it sends most teams in the wrong direction.

What the top-NRR companies have figured out is that the preference for a rep-free experience applies to the initial evaluation and purchase, not to the ongoing expansion relationship. Buyers want to get in without friction. They do not want to be sold to before they have experienced value. But the accounts that actually expand, the ones that go from a 5-seat pilot to a 50-seat deployment, that add a second product line, that evolve into enterprise contracts, almost always have a meaningful human relationship embedded in them somewhere.

The companies chasing a purely self-serve model and removing all human touchpoints are optimizing for the preference that governs purchase entry. They are not building the relationship that drives NRR above 110%. Self-service gets buyers in. Human expansion infrastructure keeps them and grows them.

## Three Expansion Motions That Actually Compound

There is no universal expansion playbook. The right motion depends on your product architecture, your pricing model, and your customer segment. But most durable expansion programs share three structural elements.

[INFOGRAPHIC HERE]

**Usage-based triggers**: If your product has any usage dimension, whether that is seats, API calls, data volume, events tracked, or actions processed, you have natural upsell signals hiding inside your product data. Companies that build automated alert and threshold systems into their customer lifecycle, flagging when customers approach limits or when usage patterns indicate readiness for the next tier, consistently outperform those that wait for manual quarterly business reviews to surface expansion conversations. The timing advantage alone is significant. A customer who gets a thoughtful outreach the week they hit 80% of their usage cap is in a very different conversation than one who gets it after they have already figured out a workaround.

**Multi-product sequencing**: The fastest path to 110%+ NRR for most SaaS businesses is selling a second product into existing accounts. Historically, this required significant platform investment. The structural advantage of this motion is that your customer already trusts you, already has your contracts in their system, already has your product embedded in their workflow. A second product lands in a fraction of the time and at a fraction of the cost of your primary product's acquisition. The trick is sequencing: leading with the right adjacent product at the right moment in the customer lifecycle, not every adjacent product at every renewal.

**Seat expansion architecture**: For seat-based pricing models, the expansion question is whether you have built the right internal champion relationships to drive organic adoption from one team to adjacent ones. Customer success teams that proactively track seat utilization and engage low-adoption accounts, not to rescue them from churn risk but to drive the adoption that creates internal word-of-mouth and natural demand for more seats, consistently generate higher NRR than teams that only engage at renewal windows.

## What Your Marketing Team Should Be Building Now

Most marketing teams define their role as ending at the handoff to sales, or at best, at the moment of close. In an expansion-led growth model, that is roughly halfway through the job.

The programs that drive NRR look like demand generation, but they point at existing customers. Content that helps your installed base extract more value from the product. Lifecycle email sequences that surface relevant features at the right moment in a customer's usage journey. In-app messaging that connects usage data to upsell conversations precisely when a customer has demonstrated they are ready. Customer community programs that create peer-to-peer advocacy loops, which both reduce voluntary churn and generate warm expansion introductions.

None of this requires marketing to abandon top-of-funnel acquisition work. It requires recognizing that any effective SaaS growth strategy runs two programs in parallel and that, in most SaaS businesses, the acquisition program is massively over-resourced relative to the expansion program.

Marketing's most important structural move in 2026 is making NRR a shared metric. Not "we handed the customer to CS and CS owns NRR." Shared. Marketing's content programs, lifecycle sequences, community investments, and in-app campaigns all feed directly into whether that number grows. The teams that have made this shift are not just better at marketing. They are structurally better businesses.

## The NRR Slide Is Your Warning Label

The four-point decline in median SaaS NRR from 2021 to 2024, moving from roughly 105% to 101% across private SaaS companies, did not happen because companies got careless. It happened because two structural forces arrived simultaneously. Post-pandemic tool rationalization pushed buyers to consolidate the software stacks they had over-purchased during the remote work boom. And early AI displacement began absorbing the low-switching-cost categories, where lightweight SaaS tools were either replaced by broader platforms or undercut by AI-native alternatives built on far smaller teams.

Those forces are not pausing. AI-native competitors are pricing more aggressively, moving faster into workflows, and making the switching cost calculation harder for your customers every quarter. The companies sitting at 101% median NRR today are not in a stable position. They are in the early innings of a slow-moving problem that accelerates whenever the next platform shift arrives.

The companies building toward [113% NRR as McKinsey documents](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/the-net-revenue-retention-advantage-driving-success-in-b2b-tech) are not spending more on acquisition. They are treating expansion as a first-class growth motion, investing in the infrastructure to support it, and making retention a shared responsibility across product, customer success, and marketing. The data is not subtle about what that decision is worth.

The companies building durable SaaS businesses in 2026 aren't the ones with the most aggressive acquisition engines; they're the ones that learned to grow through the customers they already have.
